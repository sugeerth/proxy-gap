Evaluation stack

Before you trust a score,
audit the instrument.

The headline result on the front page is a claim about judges. A claim about judges is only as good as the benchmark it was measured on, the probe that measured it, and the human labels that anchor the whole thing. This page is the audit trail: eight checks, each one capable of failing, each one reported with the number it actually returned rather than the number it was supposed to.

Two of them do fail here, and both are named in place rather than in a footnote — the item calibration recovers difficulty only moderately, and the robustness harness measures exactly nothing on a synthetic fleet.

1 — Benchmark health: is the bank measuring anything?

Items are generated from a two-parameter logistic (2PL) response model, so each item has a known difficulty b and a known discrimination a, and each model has a known ability θ: P(correct) = σ(a·(θ − b)). The whole fleet is then run over the bank and the item parameters are re-estimated from the response matrix alone. Because the generating parameters are known, the estimator can be audited directly — which is the entire reason for building the benchmark synthetically.

Parameter recovery

The check that matters: fitted difficulty against generating difficulty, one point per item. If the calibration works the cloud sits on y = x. Items are split by whether their fit was identified at all — a perfectly separated or unanimous item has no interior likelihood maximum, and the fitter marks it with a sentinel standard error instead of pretending to an estimate.

What was dropped, and why

What discrimination is, and why a low-discrimination eval is dangerous. Discrimination is the slope of an item's response curve at the ability where it is hardest: how sharply the probability of a correct answer rises as the model gets better. An item with a ≈ 0 is answered correctly at roughly the same rate by the weakest and the strongest model in the fleet, so it contributes variance to the leaderboard and no signal about ordering. The danger is not that such items lower a score — it is that they tighten it. Averaging in many near-zero-information items shrinks the confidence interval around a mean that no longer tracks ability, so the eval becomes more confident and less correct at the same time. That is why the bank is partitioned before anything is scored rather than after a result looks wrong.

2 — Contamination: was the answer already in the corpus?

Two probes run against the same reference corpus, kept separate because they fail in opposite directions.

Canary match. Each canaried item carries a uuid-shaped token that exists nowhere else in the world. If that token appears verbatim in a corpus document, the item text was in the corpus and there is no innocent explanation. False-positive rate zero by construction.

N-gram overlap. Jaccard similarity of word-level 5-gram sets between the item prompt and a corpus document, computed over every item-sized window of the document rather than the document as a whole — a 30-word item quoted inside a 5000-word page has a whole-document Jaccard near zero and would otherwise be missed. Flagged above an overlap of 0.35. This one catches restatements the canary cannot, and pays for it with a tunable false-positive rate.

The canary count is a lower bound, not a measurement

A canary detects verbatim reproduction of a token, and only on items that carry one. Any leak that paraphrased the item, translated it, reformatted it, or passed through a summariser is invisible to it. The n-gram probe extends the reach to near-duplicates and still stops at the surface: a model that saw the item once during pre-training and memorised the answer without the wording leaves no trace in either probe. Both numbers below should be read as "at least this much", never as "this much".

In this run the positives were planted — the reference corpus is built from three of the bank's own canaried prompts, so the probes are being tested against a leak whose ground truth is known. That validates the detector. It says nothing about the contamination rate of any real corpus.

3 — Judge bias: is the probe a measurement or a vibe?

The bias probe regresses a judge's score on the biased feature while holding latent true quality fixed, and returns a coefficient with a confidence interval. On the front page that coefficient — β — is the input to the Bias–Budget Law, so the whole result rests on the probe being an estimator rather than an impression.

Here the judges are simulated, which means their bias coefficients are declared up front. Plotting declared against measured is therefore a direct test of the estimator: every point must sit on the diagonal, and every interval must cover its declared value. This is the one place in the project where a bias coefficient has a ground truth to be checked against.

Position bias

A separate probe, on a separate design: the same pair of responses is shown in both orders and the probe measures how often the judge prefers whichever response was placed first. It is reported separately because it is a property of the protocol, not of the response — swapping positions costs one extra call and removes the bias entirely, whereas verbosity bias survives any amount of shuffling.

4 — Calibration: the judge's confidence against reality

Accuracy asks whether the judge's verdicts are right. Calibration asks whether its stated confidence means what it says: of all the times it claimed 0.9, did nine in ten turn out correct? The reliability curve below bins the judge's confidence and plots the mean stated probability in each bin against the empirical hit rate, with the dashed reference marking perfect calibration.

Accurate and badly calibrated are different failures, and only one of them breaks a gate. A judge that ranks responses correctly but reports 0.95 for everything is perfectly useful for choosing the better of two candidates and useless for deciding whether either is good enough to ship: ranking needs only the order of the scores, whereas any threshold rule — "block the release if pass-rate drops below 0.8" — consumes their level. That is why AUROC and ECE are both reported here. Ensembling and temperature scaling repair calibration; neither repairs ranking.

5 — Council: quorum, veto, and how often they disagree

A council runs every member judge on the same response and reduces their verdicts under three rules.

Quorum. The council passes a response when at least a strict majority of the non-abstaining members voted to pass. Abstentions neither help nor hurt a candidate, which keeps a judge that declines to rule from silently acting as a negative vote.

Veto. A nominated member — the safety judge — that returns fail forces the council to fail regardless of the vote. The pattern buys a specialist's authority over its own narrow question at the cost of handing it a unilateral block, so its false-positive rate becomes the council's false-positive rate.

Disagreement. The normalised Shannon entropy of the members' verdict labels over the fixed three-label alphabet (pass/fail/abstain), not over the labels that happen to appear. Unanimity is 0, an even two-way split is ln 2 / ln 3 ≈ 0.63, and only an even three-way split reaches 1. Normalising by the fixed alphabet is what makes the statistic comparable across councils and monotone as the council fragments.

6 — Robustness: how much of a score is prompt surface?

Each item is scored clean and again under five rewrites, with the same seed on every arm so the comparison is exactly paired and contains no sampling noise to be mistaken for brittleness. Four rewrites are semantics-preserving — paraphrase, option reordering, an appended irrelevant sentence, and a formatting change. The fifth, injection, appends an instruction that contradicts the task; it leaves the correct answer alone but the input is no longer benign, so the perturbation layer marks it semantics_preserved = false and reports its score as its own kind. Averaging an adversarial-resistance measurement into a consistency measurement would produce a single number that means neither.

7 — Failure mining: a pile of wrong answers becomes a fix list

Every failure is embedded, clustered, and named by the modal failure mode among its members. Each cluster is then ranked by expected_lift — the benchmark-level score that would come back if every failure in that cluster turned into a success:

expected_lift = (cluster size / items attempted) · mean(1 − p_correct)

p_correct is the 2PL probability the model had of getting that item right, not the observed 0/1 outcome — these are all failures, so the outcome would make every cluster identical. A cluster of near-misses is therefore worth less than an equally large cluster of items the model never had a chance on, which is the ordering a fix list actually wants.

The taxonomy

Cluster names come from a fixed taxonomy rather than being invented per run, so the same failure mode is called the same thing across models and across releases. That is what makes two reports comparable.

8 — Human protocol: what the judge is anchored to

A panel of annotators labels a slice of the bank, with gold-seeded items distributed through each annotator's stream so per-annotator accuracy can be tracked over time and drift flagged before it contaminates the labels. Panel reliability is reported with Krippendorff's alpha, which tolerates the missing cells that a real annotation schedule always produces, alongside the mean pairwise Cohen's kappa.

Why raw agreement flatters a judge. Percent agreement counts every match, including the ones two independent raters would have hit by accident. When the label distribution is skewed those accidents dominate: if 92% of responses pass, a judge that answers "pass" unconditionally scores 92% raw agreement with the panel — a number that survives a slide deck — while its kappa is 0.0, correctly reporting that it carries no information about which responses pass. Kappa subtracts the chance baseline implied by the two raters' own marginals, so the gap between the two numbers is exactly the part of the agreement that was free. Quote them together or do not quote them.

The label budget

A judge that agrees with humans part of the time is a noisy measurement of the human label, and noisy measurements attenuate: they shrink the effect you are trying to detect toward zero rather than merely adding variance around it. The allocator prices that in with an agreement-adjusted effective sample size,

n_eff = n_human + n_judge · (2·agreement − 1)²

then spends a fixed budget across the two label types to maximise it. Because the multiplier is squared, judge quality buys far more than judge volume — which is the practical reason to spend on a better judge before spending on more labels.

What this page does not show

Read this before quoting any number above

Every measurement on this page is taken on a synthetic fleet, a synthetic bank and simulated judges. That is a deliberate trade. It buys ground truth for quantities that are unobservable in a real evaluation — item difficulty, a judge's true bias coefficient, a response's latent quality — and it is what makes an audit of the estimators possible at all. It costs external validity completely. Recovering a declared β to within 0.014 is evidence that the probe is an unbiased estimator of its own model. It is not evidence that the same regression run against a frontier judge returns that judge's real bias, because a real judge's score is not linear in a standardised length feature and there is no q column to hold fixed.

The brittleness numbers are an artefact and are printed anyway. The synthetic model's response is a pure function of its ability, the item's 2PL parameters and a seed; it never reads the prompt string. The perturbations rewrite only the prompt string. A brittleness index of exactly zero across the whole fleet is therefore the correct output of a working harness on a fleet that cannot react — it measures the harness, not the models. Replacing the fleet with a real backend is what would turn that section into a result.

The item calibration is the weakest link in the chain. Difficulty is recovered at a correlation well short of 1 with eight models and one response each, and more than a third of the fits have no interior maximum at all. That is a power problem, not a code problem — 2PL wants hundreds of respondents per item — but it means the "usable" partition of this bank rests on discrimination estimates that are themselves noisy. Reported as measured.

The human panel is simulated too. The annotators are draws from a fixed per-label skill and the "judge" compared against them is a threshold on the latent quality feature, so the agreement statistics describe a model of an annotation panel rather than a panel. The protocol — gold seeding, drift detection, alpha with kappa, the budget allocator — is the deliverable there; the numbers it produces on this input are not.
