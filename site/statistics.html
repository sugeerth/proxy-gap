Statistics

Is the difference
real?

Two failures shape applied evaluation, and they are the same failure seen from opposite sides. A regression can be reported that is not there — a dashboard watching twenty independent metrics at 5% raises at least one alarm on a perfectly healthy candidate about two times in three. A regression that is there can be missed — a 200-item-per-arm eval cannot resolve an effect below about 0.28 per-item score standard deviations at 80% power. Both of those are arithmetic about the procedures, not measurements of anyone's eval suite.

Neither failure needs new methodology. Both are addressed by machinery that has been standard for decades and is often not wired into eval pipelines. This page runs that machinery on simulated data whose ground truth is known, so its operating characteristics can be checked rather than asserted.

1 — Power and the minimum detectable effect

The question a research scientist gets asked constantly is "how many items do I need to detect a one-point difference?", and as posed it has no answer. A point of a benchmark score is not a unit of anything until you divide it by the per-item score standard deviation, and the item count depends on the design as much as on the effect. Name the effect in SD units and name the design, and the arithmetic is closed-form and takes no simulation at all.

The second panel is the same fact read the other way. Power is the probability that a real difference of the target size is actually detected, and below the conventional 0.80 target you are running a test that mostly fails silently — the result comes back "not significant" and is filed as "no regression".

2 — Sequential monitoring: peeking, legally

A fixed-n test is valid if you look at it once. Eval dashboards are looked at continuously, and "run the t-test after each new item, ship when p < 0.05" is a peeking machine: the type-I error is whatever the number of looks makes it, not the number printed on the test.

An e-value is the honest alternative. It is a test martingale — a Bayes factor for "there is an effect" against "there is none" — so under the null it has expectation 1 and Ville's inequality gives P(∃n : En ≥ 1/α) ≤ α simultaneously over every stopping rule. You may peek after every single observation, stop when you like, and continue after a non-rejection. Nothing has to be declared in advance, including the sample size. A t-test permits none of that.

One convention first, because the sign is load-bearing. Δ̂ below is the running mean of the paired differences taken as first arm minus second, and the effect stream is built in report/export.py by adding a +0.12 mean shift to the second arm. The negative Δ̂ reported here is therefore the effect, not a regression. §6 orients its deltas the other way round — candidate minus baseline — and labels every column accordingly.

3 — Multiplicity: the dashboard that generates alarms

Twenty metrics, three of which carry a real shift, seventeen of which are pure noise. Each is tested with a paired permutation test. At α = 0.05 per metric the seventeen null metrics carry 0.85 expected false positives, and at least one arrives with probability 1 − 0.9517 = 58%. Exactly one arrives here — which illustrates that rate rather than estimating it.

4 — Variance reduction: power you already paid for

CUPED is the least exotic idea on this page and the highest-leverage. If you hold a covariate x that is correlated with the outcome and untouched by the treatment — the item's difficulty, the baseline model's score on that same item, last week's value of the metric — then some of the variance in your outcome is variance you already knew about, and you can subtract it:

y_adj = y − θ·(x − mean x) θ = Cov(y, x) / Var(x)

The mean is unchanged exactly, so the estimand is unchanged; the variance falls by the squared sample correlation. This is not a modelling assumption that could be wrong — it is an algebraic identity about residuals. The cost is one extra column in your results table.

5 — Clustering: why per-item intervals lie

Eval items are almost never independent at the row level. They arrive as near-duplicate pairs, as twenty instantiations of one prompt template, as every question generated from a single source document, as a whole batch labelled by one annotator. Within a group the errors share a component, and the shared component does not average away as you add rows from that same group.

Treating those rows as independent divides the standard error by √N when the design only supports something closer to √G. The interval comes out too narrow, the p-value too small, and the eval "detects" an improvement that is really one document having a good day. The error is anticonservative — it manufactures significance rather than losing it — which is why it survives review: nobody questions a result that came out the way they hoped.

The fix is the Liang–Zeger sandwich with the CR1 small-sample correction G/(G−1), applied at the cluster level, with the permutation test flipping signs per cluster rather than per item. It assumes nothing about the within-cluster correlation structure, and it is consistent in the number of clusters — not the number of rows. Adding items to an existing document buys you almost nothing; adding documents buys you precision.

6 — The CI gate

Everything above exists to serve one decision: does this candidate ship? A gate is a decision procedure, not a report, and the property that decides whether it survives contact with a real release train is not its sensitivity. It is its false-alarm rate.

The design principle. A gate that cries wolf gets disabled within a week, and a disabled gate protects nothing at all. If a suite watches m independent metrics and tests each at its own α, the chance that at least one fires on a perfectly healthy candidate is 1 − (1 − α)m — at m = 20, roughly two releases in three. Controlling the error rate across the family is therefore not statistical fastidiousness. It is the only thing that buys the gate enough credibility to be allowed to block anything.

Blocking here requires two independent conditions to hold at once: the evidence must survive Benjamini–Hochberg (q ≤ α) and the whole 95% interval must sit below the tolerated regression. Significance alone does not block, and a negative point estimate alone does not block. Two scenarios are run — one where nothing changed, one where a single metric was really moved.

What this page does not establish

Read this before quoting any number above

Every stream, metric and comparison here is simulated. The data are drawn from the generators in proxygap.report.export, which is what makes ground truth available — you know which three of the twenty metrics really moved, and you know the null stream is really null. That is the point of doing it this way, and it is also the limit: nothing here measures the operating characteristics of a real eval suite on real model outputs.

These are single realisations, not rate estimates. The null e-value stream's false alarm and the one spurious metric in §3 each illustrate a rate; neither estimates one. A single run cannot distinguish a 5% procedure from a 15% one. The repository's test suite is where the realised type-I rates are measured over thousands of streams (tests/test_sequential.py); this page shows one draw from the seed recorded in site/data/manifest.json.

The designs are not interchangeable. §1 is two-sample arithmetic; §2 and §6 are paired. Feeding an unpaired score SD into a paired design overstates the items you need. Feeding a paired difference SD into the two-sample formulas overstates the MDE by exactly √2: the two-sample standard error is sd·√(2/n) where the paired one is sd_d/√n, so the number that comes back is √2 larger than the effect the design can really resolve, and a paired eval reads as blinder than it is. Both mistakes are common, both are conservative in the direction that hides them, and neither is visible in the output.

Each method has a stated failure mode. The e-value's exactness assumes i.i.d. differences and survives Student-t, binary and uniform departures, but strongly skewed differences break it — a centred lognormal null stream rejects far above its nominal level, and that case wants a bounded-support empirical-Bernstein e-value instead. Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false discovery rate, not the family-wise error rate; the two coincide under the global null, which is the case a gate must survive, and diverge elsewhere. Cluster-robust standard errors are consistent as the number of clusters grows, and the gate here has 25 of them, which is not many.
